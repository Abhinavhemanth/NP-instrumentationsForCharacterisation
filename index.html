How do we find out what a nanoparticle really is?

You cannot see a 20 nm particle with your eyes, so scientists use instruments that each answer a different question. Read a short guide, then walk around six of them in 3D.

Explore the 3D instruments or read the guide first

Why nanoparticles must be characterised

Nanoparticles are particles with at least one dimension between about 1 and 100 nm. At this size, properties depend strongly on size, shape, crystal structure, composition and surface chemistry, so two samples of the same material can behave very differently.

An example

Gold is yellow and unreactive as a bulk metal. As 20 nm particles in water it is ruby red, and it becomes a catalyst. Change the particles to rods or make them clump together, and the colour changes again.

Surface area also grows quickly as particles shrink. A larger share of the atoms sits on the surface, which is why reactivity, melting point and toxicity can all change.

What characterisation gives you

It links how a sample was made to how it behaves. It shows whether a synthesis worked, whether the next batch matches the last one, and whether a particle is suitable for a catalyst, sensor, drug carrier or solar cell.

No single instrument answers everything, so researchers combine several of them.

Which question, which instrument

QuestionPropertyTypical technique
How big are the particles?Size and size distributionTEM, SEM, AFM; XRD gives crystallite size
What do they look like?Shape, morphology, aggregationSEM, TEM, AFM
Are they crystalline, and which phase?Crystal structure, lattice spacingXRD, TEM electron diffraction
What coats the surface?Capping agents, functional groupsFTIR
Did they form, and are they stable?Optical properties, plasmon band, band gapUV-Vis spectroscopy
What are they made of?Elemental compositionEDS (with SEM or TEM), XPS

Other common methods, such as dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential, BET surface area and TGA, are not in the viewer yet.

The six instruments

Each one has a 3D model you can rotate, pull apart and watch working. Click “Open in 3D” to go straight to it.

SEM · Scanning Electron Microscope

Scans a focused electron beam over the surface and builds an image from the electrons that come back.

Gives:
surface morphology, particle shape, agglomeration
Watch for:
sample must be dry and conductive or coated
Open in 3D

AFM · Atomic Force Microscope

A tiny tip on a flexible cantilever feels the surface while a laser tracks its bending.

Gives:
3D height map, true particle height, roughness
Watch for:
slow, and the tip shape widens features sideways
Open in 3D

TEM · Transmission Electron Microscope

Electrons pass through an ultra-thin sample and are magnified by electromagnetic lenses.

Gives:
size, shape, lattice fringes, electron diffraction
Watch for:
needs very thin samples; expensive
Open in 3D

XRD · X-ray Diffractometer

X-rays diffract from crystal planes at angles given by Bragg’s law, nλ = 2d sinθ.

Gives:
crystal phase, lattice parameters, crystallite size
Watch for:
an average over the whole powder
Open in 3D

FTIR · Infrared Spectrometer

Bonds absorb infrared light at their own vibration frequencies; a Fourier transform turns the signal into a spectrum.

Gives:
functional groups, capping agents, surface chemistry
Watch for:
no size information; water interferes
Open in 3D

UV-Vis · Spectrophotometer

Measures how much light a sample absorbs at each wavelength, following the Beer–Lambert law.

Gives:
plasmon peak (Au ≈ 520 nm, Ag ≈ 400 nm), band gap, concentration
Watch for:
size is only indirect
